## Part 1 — The executive order: VERIFIED

Grok's opening factual claim was **correct on every point**, and it was checked against primary sources.

**Executive Order 14434, "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence," signed 29 September 2026.**

| Source | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| `whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/09/inaugurating-the-era-of-super-intelligence` | Official text, published 2026-09-29 |
| **Federal Register, Vol. 91, No. 190, Friday 2 October 2026, pp. 63129–63130** | **FR Doc No: 2026-20321** — the permanent record |
| `presidency.ucsb.edu` (American Presidency Project) | Archival text |
| White House fact sheet | Confirms the APST directive |

**Grok's three assertions, each confirmed in the text:**

1. ✓ Adopts **"Super Intelligence" / "SI"** as the preferred term across the executive branch — §2(a):
   *"shall use 'Super Intelligence' and 'SI' in place of 'Artificial Intelligence' and 'AI' in official
   correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, policy documents, and other non-statutory
   documents within the executive branch."*
2. ✓ Goes further than a style memo — §1: the branch *"will not acknowledge the usage of 'Artificial
   Intelligence' and 'AI' in any applicable setting."*
3. ✓ Directs the **APST**, within **60 days**, to submit **proposed legislative language** establishing a
   federal definition — §3(b), with three required components.

**⚠️ One item still open.** 15 U.S.C. § 9401(3) — the definition the order borrows — is quoted in commentary
sources as *"a machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, make predictions,
recommendations or decisions influencing real or virtual environments."* **The primary text at
uscode.house.gov has not been read directly.** Verify before print. One fetch closes it.

## Part 2 — ⭐ What Grok missed, and it corrects the Introduction

**Grok's framing: "name first, definition later."** That is the weaker version, and it is the version the
current Introduction draft carries:

> *"The order directs the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology to propose a Federal
> definition of 'Super Intelligence.' Read that again. **The name was enacted before the definition was
> written.**"*

**The order does not leave the term undefined.** It defines it in §3(a) — **as exactly the thing it claims to
surpass:**

> **§3(a):** *"For purposes of this order, and except where otherwise provided by law, the terms 'Super
> Intelligence' and 'SI' **mean the technologies and systems encompassed by the term 'artificial
> intelligence' as defined in section 9401(3) of title 15, United States Code.** This definition shall
> govern the implementation of this order unless and until superseded..."*

**So set §1 against §3(a), in the order's own words:**

| §1 (the claim) | §3(a) (the definition) |
|---|---|
| *"far exceed what was envisioned when the term 'Artificial Intelligence' first came into use"* | *"'Super Intelligence' and 'SI' mean ... 'artificial intelligence' as defined in section 9401(3) of title 15"* |
| *"not merely artificial intelligence, but a new era of Super Intelligence"* | the same statute, cited |
| *"more appropriately captures the promise, potential, and rapidly advancing capabilities"* | **identical scope, by reference** |

**The order refutes itself between its own sections.** Section 1 asserts a capability; Section 3 legally
disclaims it. **The name changed. The thing did not.** And §2(b) confirms it: *"Nothing in this section
requires the alteration of previously issued regulations, Presidential actions, contracts, grants, or other
historical documents."*

### The correction to the Introduction

**Replace:** *"The name was enacted before the definition was written."*

**With the verifiable and sharper claim:** the order **did** define the new name — as the old definition,
word for word by reference — and simultaneously commissioned a *different* federal definition within 60 days.
**A name was granted a capability, and the law defining that name disclaims the capability in the same
document.**

### Why this matters beyond the wording

- **It is stronger.** "No definition yet" invites *"well, one is coming."* **"The definition is the old one"
  cannot be answered that way.**
- **It is permanent.** §3(a) is in the **Federal Register**, dated, page-numbered. It satisfies the book's own
  rule — *never build on a version number* — because the text of a signed EO does not churn. The *event*
  dates; **the self-contradiction is fixed and quotable.**
- **It is the thesis, self-argued.** The book claims the error is crediting a system with an intelligence its
  own definitions say it has not met. **A government did exactly that, in a document anyone can read.**

### ⚠️ Adapted, not accepted — the "absolute formulation" critique

His argument: many models **are** reliable on narrow, high-frequency, low-stakes facts; so the stronger claim
is that *"the model has no internal way to mark the difference between retrieval and construction"* and the
user takes *"a risk that scales with the cost of being wrong."*

**Two things are true here:**

1. **The alternative he proposes is already the book's position.** Chapter 2's claim is that a real memory
   and a confabulation are *"indistinguishable from the outside — and from the inside."* That **is** "no
   internal way to mark the difference." **He is restating the book's own rule as a correction to it.**
2. ⚠️ **Softening to "risk scales with cost" hands the reader a license** — *if it's low-stakes, trust it* —
   which is the exact habit the book indicts. The rule is a **policy**, not a claim about frequency.

**⭐ The distinction that keeps both:**

> **The rule is not "it is always wrong" — a statistical claim about frequency, which is falsifiable and
> therefore softenable. The rule is "you cannot tell which case you are in" — an epistemic claim about
> observability, which is absolute and which survives every counter-example.**

**And his demo suggestion is better than his argument for it.** Showing the invented case *beside* the
ordinary-fact case does not weaken the absolute — **it demonstrates it.** Two outputs, one invented and one
correct, arriving in the same fluent form with no signal which is which. **The reader sees the problem
instead of being told about it.** Take the demo; keep the rule.

### ⭐ Accepted, and it is his best paragraph — the division of labour

> *"The book is most persuasive when it stays with the division of labor (facts and stakes on the human side;
> transformation, checking against supplied material, and refusal to invent on the model side) and least

**Correct, and the draft already avoids the trap** — *"AI is already smart enough"* is the opposite of

> *"continuing to treat the model-written chapters as **inspectable records rather than as testimony about

**"Inspectable records, not testimony about the interior"** — that is the book's *never build on a version
number* rule applied to its own chapters.
