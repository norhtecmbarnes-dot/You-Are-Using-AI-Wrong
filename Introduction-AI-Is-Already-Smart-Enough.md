# Introduction — AI Is Already Smart Enough

**STATUS: draft.** Placed after the Preface and before *The Rules*. This is the book's claim, stated
before the argument for it begins.

---

The naming debate is worth two paragraphs, and then we can put it down.

On 29 September 2026 the President signed an executive order replacing **"Artificial Intelligence"**
with **"Super Intelligence" — "SI"** across the executive branch: official correspondence, public
communications, policy documents. Agencies were directed to use the new terms and **to no longer
acknowledge the old ones at all.** The stated reasoning was that *"super"* conveys *"the true capabilities
of the technologies being developed today… not simply imitating or automating human intelligence."*

I am not going to argue about the word. I am going to point at what the word *claims* — and at one detail
inside the order itself.

**The order directs the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology to propose a Federal
definition of "Super Intelligence."**

And it defines the term itself, in section 3(a). Here is that sentence, and it is worth reading slowly:

> *"For purposes of this order, and except where otherwise provided by law, the terms 'Super
> Intelligence' and 'SI' mean the technologies and systems encompassed by the term 'artificial
> intelligence' as defined in section 9401(3) of title 15, United States Code."*

**Super Intelligence means artificial intelligence.** Not something beyond it — the same statute, cited
by number. Section 1 of that same order says these systems *"far exceed what was envisioned when the term
'Artificial Intelligence' first came into use"* and are *"not merely artificial intelligence."* Section 3
says they are precisely artificial intelligence, as already defined in law.

**And it is worse than that, once you read the definition it borrows.** Title 15, section 9401(3) defines
artificial intelligence as *"a machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives,
make predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing real or virtual environments."* **There is no
intelligence threshold in it.** No measure of capability, no comparison to a person, nothing that a
spreadsheet macro could not satisfy. So "Super Intelligence", as the order defines it, means **a machine
that makes predictions** — which is the opposite of what the word *superintelligence* means in the
literature the order is borrowing from.

**A name was granted a capability, and the section defining the name does not even ask for it.** The document
argues with itself, and it does so in two paragraphs that sit four pages apart.

*Primary sources: whitehouse.gov, 29 September 2026; Federal Register vol. 91 no. 190, 2 October 2026,
pp. 63129–63130, FR Doc 2026-20321; 15 U.S.C. § 9401(3), read at law.cornell.edu and law.justia.com.*

That inversion is the entire subject of this book. **A word was chosen to fit the technology, rather than
the technology being measured against a word** — and it is the same move that put *"ask me anything"* on
the front of a text engine, and the same move that has organisations trusting reports nobody can check.
The capability is asserted first and defined afterward.

**"Superintelligence" is not a new term and it does not mean "very good software."** It was defined by
Nick Bostrom in 2014 as *any intellect that greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in
virtually all domains of interest.* It sits deliberately **a step beyond** artificial general
intelligence — and AGI, by OpenAI's own definition, means highly autonomous systems that outperform humans
at most economically valuable work. Today's products are neither. By the definition their own makers
publish, you cannot yet hire one as a reliable virtual employee.

So the rename is not a correction of an unflattering word. **It is a promotion** — the technology granted
a claim about itself that its own definitions say it has not met. And that is the error this entire book
is about: **crediting the system with an intelligence it does not have, immediately before asking it to
do your work.**

The order is cited here in its own words, from the White House fact sheet of 29 September 2026. It is a
public document, and it is worth reading directly rather than through anyone's description of it —
which, as the rest of this book will argue, is the only way to read anything.

### What the money is betting on

The rename is cosmetic. The money is not.

Enormous sums are being invested on the premise that **the next model will be the one that works** — that
a sufficiently general intelligence is coming, and beyond it, an intelligence no human can outthink. The
bet is not that we will learn to use these systems well. The bet is that they will get smart enough that
we will not have to.

**This book says that premise is wrong, and the money is aimed at the wrong problem.**

### Why this book still says "AI"

Not out of nostalgia, and not to reject the argument that the technology deserves a better name. It says
"AI" because **the intelligence was never the part that was failing.**

If you are waiting for a smarter model to fix what is not working, it is worth being exact about what you
would be trusting in the meantime. Ask a system to replace a person's judgement and you are relying on an
intelligence that:

- **never learns** from you — the weights do not move, and a new conversation starts from the same place,
  remembering nothing;
- **never updates** — its knowledge is frozen at the moment it finished training and goes stale from there,
  some of it in weeks;
- **never improves** — it is the same model on the thousandth question as on the first;
- **answers in a different voice depending on the question's tone**, and on settings you probably did not
  choose — temperature, seed — none of which change what it knows, only which answer you get;
- and arrives already **shaped by human feedback**: biases its trainers did not intend, guardrails that
  let it be wrong by omission, and a documented tendency to agree with you.

That is not a description of a colleague. **It is a description of a very fluent instrument** — and it
explains why a bigger model does not fix the problem, only makes the wrong answers more convincing.

### The one claim this book makes

> **AI at its current state can do a great deal of useful work — and it must never be treated as a source
> of knowledge.**

That is the theme, and every chapter is an argument for it.

**The tools are already good enough.** Organisations and individuals are not failing with AI because the
model is too small, or because superintelligence has not arrived. They are failing because they are
**using it wrong**: asking a language engine for facts instead of handing it facts, trusting fluent
confident answers as though fluency were evidence, and buying the largest model for every task because it
feels safer.

**Knowledge can come from an AI — but only when you bypass its training.** Give it the documents. Have it
research and cite. Make it read instead of remember. Then it is doing the thing it is genuinely excellent
at, and you are getting knowledge with a provenance you can check.

Everything that follows is how to build that. And it does not require a smarter model, more money, or a
new name.

## How this book was made

**This is not a book written by AI. It is a book written *with* AI.** The distinction is the whole of it.

My partner in this work is an open-source model running on my own machine. **It created none of these rules. It did none of the research. It vouched for nothing out of its own knowledge.** What it did was hold me to the rules — to keep me honest in the approach. **Every assertion in this book traces back to a document it read, not to something it decided.**

The method was simple, and it was not a prompt trick. I gave it a standing instruction: *every statement is backed by fact and research; if you cannot show where it came from, say so; if my claim is unsupported, say so; do not agree with me to be agreeable.* Then it did the thing I could not do at scale — **it read what I had written and asked me to justify it.** Forty-one sources were checked. Two of the claims I brought it were rejected. And when I told it about my own experience, **it asked me for witnesses.**

That is where one of the rules in this book came from — the rule that a person can be the source for their own experience — **together with the companion understanding that not all information is equally important.** Not everything a human says needs to be verified. Most of it is context, illustration, or a joke, and a model has no way to tell the difference unless you tell it.

**This book is itself a model of the thing it describes.** It took many hours across many sessions. The rules did not all exist when it began; **several were written in the middle of the work, because the work exposed what was missing.** That was the arrangement throughout. I brought the knowledge and the judgement. The model brought the checking. **It did not know more than I do. It would not let me be sloppy.**

**It is not a book about AGI, or SI, or the next thing.** It is about working with a model whose results you **question** rather than being **seduced** — by its knowledge, or by its verbosity. Those are two different habits, and the second one is the one that costs people money.

**Nor is it a book about prompting.** There is no prompt list here. **It is not about making a model perform better.** It is about improving how the *human* works with the model:

- **questioning the output instead of being impressed by it**
- **getting the most done for the least amount of money**
- **improving processes — and doing things differently — to take real advantage of what these systems actually offer**

**That is where the value is. And it is the part no vendor can sell you.**

## Notes for the author (not part of the introduction)

**Why the SI material belongs here and why it must stay short.** You asked for it *in the discussion*, and
it earns its place for one reason: **it is the book's category error, committed at national scale and
written into the federal register.** That is a gift — the thesis arrives pre-argued by somebody else.

But three cautions:

1. **Keep it to two paragraphs, as drafted.** The moment it grows it becomes a political essay, and the
   book loses the reader who disagrees with the politics but needs the method.
2. **It dates.** Your own rule: never build on anything with a version number. An executive order is a
   version number. The chapter must still work for a reader who has never heard of the rename — which is
   why the substance (a name that *claims* a capability) carries the argument and the event merely
   illustrates it.
3. **No villany, per your standing rule.** The draft says what the order said and what the word means. It
   does not characterise motive. **Do not add a motive.** The strongest version of this passage is the
   flattest one.

**The name question is answered by the substance, not by preference.** The reason to say "AI" is not that
"SI" is a bad brand. It is that *superintelligence* is a term with a **definition**, and the definition
has not been met. That is checkable, and it is the same move the book makes everywhere else: **look at
what is actually there, not at what it is called.**

**One thing to decide.** Your other book, *Surviving the AI Apocolypse* (note: that spelling is the
published title), takes a different posture — an exposé of concentrated power and inequality rather than
this book's "no villains, only incentive-shaped design." **Point at it once and do not lean on it**, so the
two books don't argue with each other in front of the reader. Suggested wording, if you want the
cross-reference: *"I have written about the AGI question at length elsewhere. This book is about the part
that does not require waiting for it."*
⚠️ **If you use that line, confirm the AGI discussion is actually in that book** — its blurb is about
technocrats and monopolies, not superintelligence. A pointer that misfires is worse than no pointer.

**Three things on that book's Amazon listing that are worth ten minutes of your time.** I checked it while
verifying the title, and all three are metadata edits:
1. **The published title contains a misspelling** — *"Apocolypse"*. The blurb body spells it correctly,
   which is how I know it isn't an artefact of the page.
2. **The Kindle listing shows the author as "Micahel" Barnes**; the paperback shows "Michael Charles".
3. **The description text is encoding-corrupted** — *"to ét their vision"*, *"énding alternatives"*,
   *"signiécant role"*. The "fi" ligature has been mangled.

**I mention the third one plainly because this book will make the same argument about garbled text:** you
reject AI media for it. A sales page that reads *"ét"* costs you readers who would otherwise buy, and it
takes minutes to fix — which is a better trade than any of the flags in this folder.
